01 // THE PITCH

What Is It, Who's It For, and Why Play It?

We built Spin to Lose in Unity (URP 3D) for the Juniper Devs Game Jam. The jam theme was "Spin to Win," so we flipped it on its head. Set in a world where gambling is everywhere, an antagonist who lost everything through addiction has trapped gambling addicts in a chair. You're strapped in and forced to pull a slot machine lever repeatedly. The twist is that hitting the jackpot is BAD. Every jackpot triggers the antagonist to take a body part in exchange for powerups. Your goal is to NOT win—pull the lever 6 times without hitting the jackpot to unlock all 6 shackles and escape.

It's built for players who love tense, low-poly psychological horror and strategy games like Buckshot Roulette. We kept the tone psychological horror rather than gore: the SAW-inspired premise stays, but all body horror is implied through a cut to black and sound design (one thud, a cloth sound, and silence) while the antagonist stays calm, clinical, and backlit in silhouette.

02 // CORE LOOP & FIRST SESSION

In the first few minutes, you're locked in a fixed first-person POV at arm's reach of the machine. Starting jackpot probability is around 40–45% (displayed diegetically right on the machine face instead of a 2D UI overlay). You pull the lever, the 3 separate reel cylinders spin and clunk to a stop, and RNG determines the result: a non-jackpot unlocks 1 shackle, while a jackpot flickers the lights, plays a falsely triumphant major-key jingle, and brings in the antagonist to take a body part and give you powerup cards to lower your jackpot odds.

WHAT BRINGS THEM BACK TOMORROW

Two things: First, after a sacrifice you can accept medical stitching (safe, no bonus) or risk taking an extra body part for a better loot tier of cards. Second, even after all 6 shackles are unlocked and you can escape, you can still choose to stay in the chair and continue spinning—testing how far your rigged odds can carry you.

03 // PROGRESSION & METAGAME

The Day-1 Hook & What Keeps Someone Playing for Months

The Day-1 Hook I Built: The main hook in the prototype is the sacrifice choice menu (accepting medical stitching vs. offering an extra body part to pull from a higher loot tier of cards) combined with the "open the machine" tool where you pop the back panel off and mess with the internal wiring to lower your jackpot float. Plus, letting the player choose to keep spinning even after all 6 shackles are open baits them into testing their luck for one more pull.

What Keeps Someone Playing for Months: To turn this into a long-term game without a stupid long stat grind, I'd use the exact progression model I wrote about in my Obsidian analysis of Hades (the "Pact of Punishment" / Heat system). Once you beat the final boss/escape the chair once, you unlock "Heat" options (which in Hades scale from 0 to 57, with the record being 52). Core changes to your abilities can be refunded so every run starts fresh, you can equip a low-impact Keepsake, and the game pushes you to turn up the difficulty modifiers to earn upgrade currencies rather than just grinding easy runs.

04 // MONEY

How It Makes Money Without Feeling Like a Cash Grab

Since the whole premise of Spin to Lose is about the horror of gambling addiction, selling real-money revives or pay-to-win probability boosts mid-run would completely ruin the game and feel like a cash grab.

Instead, I would use the same fair F2P model I designed for Question #2:

  • Cosmetics & Mix-and-Match Skin Packs: Players can buy skin packs for the slot machine body, custom pixel-art card faces in the tray, and shackle/room cosmetics that apply across the whole game and can be mixed and matched.
  • Subtle Energy & Dual Currency: If run as a mobile F2P title, runs use a subtle energy bar system where completing runs rewards a small amount of premium currency so F2P players can earn convenience items for free.
  • Zero Forced Ads: Interrupting the horror atmosphere with forced ads mid-run ruins the tension. Ads are strictly opt-in outside of runs.
05 // AI: HOW I USED IT & WHETHER IT'S IN THE GAME

Hand-Made Jam Assets + AI for Code & Shaders

How I Used AI to Build It: We built this prototype for the Juniper Devs Game Jam, where the #1 rule was No AI art or audio (no Midjourney, DALL-E, Suno, or ElevenLabs), and the rule on code was "Code assistance is unenforceable but use your conscience." Because college ran from 7am to 1pm every day, our 3-person team only had a 6-hour work window (1pm–7pm, ~36 hours total across 6 days). All our 3D models were hand-made in Blender (low-poly, flat-shaded, no smoothing groups), all 8 reel symbols and 5 card faces were drawn in Aseprite, and all foley (lever pull, 3 reel stop clunks, shackle clicks, boots, and pitch-shifted antagonist lines) was recorded and processed in Audacity and LMMS.

I was responsible for all the code, so I used AI coding tools (Claude Code / Gemini) to help me write the ~20-line custom HLSL fullscreen posterization shader for Unity URP on Day 1, speed up the C# RNG/shackle state scripts, and build this web page. Is AI part of the game itself? No—inside the build, the slot machine runs on an exposed C# jackpot % float modified by the cards you play.

06 & 07 // SHIPPING, SOFT LAUNCH NUMBERS & REFERENCE GAMES

What We Test First, Our Cut List & Buckshot Roulette

What I'd Test First & What Makes Us Change Direction: In our Day 4 and Day 5 team playtests, the #1 thing we tested was whether the ~40–45% starting jackpot probability felt too brutal or too easy, aiming for an 8–15 minute full run. We also wrote a strict Scope Safety Net cut list in case we fell behind: (1) Cut the `Open the machine` wiring tool first (the cards-only system is still complete without it), (2) Cut mechanical body part penalties and just track them with a greyed-out icon, (3) Cut the 3D antagonist model to a flat plane + backlight shadow, (4) Cut the intro cutscene to a title card + 2 lines of text, and (5) Ship with 4 cards instead of 5. The things we never cut are the posterization shader, the core slot loop, the 6 shackles, and the sacrifice system.

Couple of Numbers to Watch at Soft Launch: Using the table from LILA's Business Values PDF (Slide 31), we watch D1 Retention (target 40%) and Average Core Loop Actions (8+ per day) at Soft Launch, then shift to Organic ARPDAU ($0.15) and D7 ROAS (20%) at Hard Launch.

Reference Games: Our main visual and vibe reference was Buckshot Roulette. We pulled apart and borrowed its 4–6 band posterization look, low-poly flat-shaded models with no anti-aliasing, 1 harsh overhead point light + 1 red/amber fill, VHS scanlines + chromatic aberration + film grain, fixed first-person POV at arm's reach, and keeping the antagonist as a backlit silhouette. What we did differently: Instead of a shotgun duel, we put you in front of a slot machine where hitting the jackpot is what kills you, and you use cards and internal wiring to lower your own odds of winning.

QUESTION #2 // GAMEPLAY INSIGHTS & STRATEGY

Interactive Narrative Sub-Genre: Story Mode Games

REINVENTING TELLTALE-STYLE STORY GAMES FOR MOBILE F2P
WHY IT HASN'T WORKED SO FAR

The Top 100 App Store "Slop" & Why Telltale Disappeared from F2P

Recently I browsed the top 100 games in the App Store just to see what was on there, and all of it was just slop. All the games were fast-paced, high-dopamine games. As a model, it's fine to have games whose entire design is just to capture the user's attention for as long as possible, but it takes the "fun" part about games in my opinion. There was a time where the App Store had good story-defined games which were actually fun and playable, that wanted more from the people who were playing it. You needed to take notes about the objectives and not have the game feed it to you.

Popular games from the Telltale series like The Walking Dead, Batman, Borderlands, etc., had an interactive story-based method, where the user's action would deeply impact the story. You needed to listen to the story as it progressed to be able to understand what was going on and influence it, but now I don't see any games that represent even 1% of what Telltale Games produced for the F2P market. It was only one ep which was free at first, but that one ep hooked so many people every single game they did. To change that, a few changes can be done to the innate design of the tailored story mode games.

TWEAK 01 // EVERY 2 MINUTES

Pacing & Quick-Time Events

We need to take into account the short attention span of people and add tweaks to the story based on that. Every 2 minutes we need to keep the viewer on the edge of the seat as he witnesses the story being played out. The more filler scenes which have more white spaces, we remove them from the story and make the calm story-like game into an interactive one which thrives with the viewer's attention.

E.g., we can take Telltale's The Walking Dead beautifully designed games, and the story is just wonderful. Keeping the story the same but adding more quick-time events and reducing the gap time between each dialogue: if Lee gets to choose from 4 options, instead of then waiting again for like 5 mins for another set to come up, cut that in half. It adds more that the player can do for the game rather than just staring at a screen and watching that cutscene happen. If we could incorporate the high dopamine-inducing activities that relate to the quick-time events with this game, we can keep the player hooked for as long as possible.

TWEAK 02 // TAILORED BY THE PLAYER

Environmental Interactivity

This format isn't particularly following the open-world concept as it's mainly guided by the story, and having only certain areas explorable. Instead of increasing the explorable area, we add more interactables for the main character.

The game is very linear as you can only interact with a few things and talk to people, that's all. But if you were to add more interactivity for the character, then it would be more fun. E.g., adding more choices of weapons in the game which you can select from, which would make the story more tailored "by the player".

FIG. 2.3 // PRODUCTION BUDGET

Lesser Budget vs. Expensive Side

Now, some of these can be expensive to the production to make. E.g., adding more events and more choices can lead to the devs making more different scenarios, which would lead to adding more budget to the game, which can't really be feasible:

FOR A LESSER BUDGET:

We can do the interactivity part of the environment, as it's just adding a few more actions that the player can do to the environment.

FOR THE EXPENSIVE SIDE:

We can do the quick-time events which tailor the story more to the player and having multiple endings and dialogues.

FREE-TO-PLAY MONETIZATION STRATEGY

10/20 Energy Bars, "Dollars" & No Forced Ads

To monetize an interactive narrative game effectively without ruining the story, we need to implement a subtle energy system. For example, starting an episode might cost 10 energy bars out of a maximum capacity of 20. Once depleted, players would need to replenish their energy either by waiting it out over time or by purchasing more from the in-game store.

This economy would rely on a dual-currency system: energy bars and a premium in-game currency (e.g., "dollars"). Energy replenishes naturally, but if a player runs out, they can buy premium dollars to purchase immediate energy refills. To maintain a fair experience for strictly F2P players who don't want to spend real money, completing an episode would reward them with a small amount of premium dollars, giving them a path to earn convenience items for free.

Crucially, there should be absolutely no forced ads during the episodes. Interrupting the story with ads completely ruins the user's immersion and interactivity. Instead, ads should be strictly opt-in, where players can choose to watch a rewarded video ad exclusively in exchange for a free energy bar.

QUESTION #3 // DESIGN SPECIFICATION

True Roguelike Synergy Builds

SCENARIO: MOBILE SURVIVOR-LIKE (SURVIVOR.IO / VAMPIRE SURVIVORS)
WHY THIS FEATURE & HOW THE BUILD SYSTEM WORKS

Making Every Run New So You Aren't Just Dodging and Running

Let's take Survivor.io as an example and to add a new interactive feature to it, I would honestly go for a roguelike-ish combat system, it still being fully automated but every run is new for the player. He would need to have a starter weapon and he'll need a build for that, so he starts off with a certain ability and we would have it so that none of the builds can ever be the same, adding that very randomness every time. It would engage the player a lot, as a survival game can get boring pretty fast, and you're not even doing the combat—you're just dodging and running. Adding a build system would be very beneficial.

If we were to go for a throwable build, we would select our starter weapons, but that only affects the nature of our primary weapon now. Kunais would lead to just a throwing base attack, but the thing about roguelikes is, your main attack can also not be your main build. We could get another overpowered missile cast that doubles after every hit and stops for 1 sec as a cooldown, and you can also go with just a high-DPS single-strike throwable build—the possibilities are endless with the build system. Implementing a roguelike system of elements on the other hand would lead the characters to even be more versatile with their build: Kunai + Water as a starter weapon, you get a silent yet deadly build and you can keep upgrading other things.
